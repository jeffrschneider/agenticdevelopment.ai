Where it is thin

Nine gaps, what each one is actually missing, and what would have to be written.

These are the places where the lifecycle does not join up. None of them is blocked on a capability. Each is blocked on something nobody has written down, which makes them a to-do list rather than a forecast.

The contract before the code

Code, Test and Review are one gap wearing three hats, and the missing thing is the same in all three: a statement of what the program must be true of, written before the program, by something that will not write the program.

With it, Code has something to be given. Test has something to test against that the program cannot quietly redefine. Review has something to hold findings against. Without it, all three collapse into one person reading carefully, which is where they sit today.

What would have to exist: a format for that statement, a rule that it precedes the program, and a rule that the hand which writes it is not the hand which satisfies it. Note that this is not a test suite. A test suite is a program, and a program written by the same hand encodes the same misunderstanding.

The statement of what it may touch

Security review has the same shape as the gap above and a worse consequence. An agent is built, and somewhere between the design and the deploy somebody decides what it may reach, what it may spend and what it must never do. Usually that decision is made by whoever writes the install file, at the moment they write it, and it is never reviewed as a decision at all.

This matters more here than in ordinary software because the thing being built acts without anybody watching. A program with too much access does damage when somebody runs it wrongly; an agent with too much access does damage on its own schedule. And the access is set before the agent ever runs, so it is one of the few things that genuinely has to be got right in advance.

What would have to exist: a statement of what a package may reach and spend, written before the credentials are bound, with a reason recorded against each one, and a rule that adding an access later is a change somebody has to approve rather than a line in a config file.

The standard before the work

Eval is missing the same kind of thing one level up. Acceptance testing is solved, because a package declares what it does and cases can be derived from the declaration mechanically. But an agent that answers correctly may still be producing work that is not worth having, and no declaration can settle that.

A mechanical check catches faults. It has no opinion about whether the work was worth doing. That opinion has to come from somewhere, and if it comes from the maker after the fact it is not worth much.

What would have to exist: a written statement of what good looks like for a job, produced before the work and by somebody other than the maker, and a role for the thing that grades against it. The nearest published role is the agent evaluator, which deliberately answers a different question and says so.

The authority to publish

Publish is the stage that makes work visible outside the system, which makes it the one stage where a mistake is public. It is usually treated as plumbing and done by whoever holds the credential.

What it actually needs is the same three things any authority needs: a statement of where this thing may write, a record of what it wrote, and a digest so that what is served can be compared with what was handed over. Everything else, including the refusal to publish something it was not given, follows from those.

What would have to exist: a role, with a bound address rather than a general credential, and a record per publication.

The account across everything

Cost is unusual on this list because the hard half is done. Ceilings per job are common, they work, and a job that would pass one stops.

The missing half is the view across all of it. Agentic spend is made of many small automatic decisions rather than a few large deliberate ones, so the per-job ceiling that protects you from one runaway job is precisely the wrong instrument for noticing that forty jobs are each spending a little more than they should. There is a published role for this, the bookkeeper, and the gap is that nothing holds it for development work.

What would have to exist: every spend attributable to a job, and something holding the bookkeeper role over the whole fleet rather than per job.

The way to stop something that is still working

Monitoring notices. Rollback fixes. Between them is the part nobody has written down, which is what to do in the minutes after something is found and before anybody knows what to change.

The problem specific to agents is that a misbehaving one does not fall over. It has an inbox, it keeps answering, and it goes on producing plausible bad work until somebody stops it. Stopping it is its own act and it is usually not available: the choices are to leave it running or to take it away entirely, and those are very different decisions to be forced into at speed.

What would have to exist: a way to stop an agent taking new work without removing it, an account of what it did while it was wrong, and a rule about who may pull that lever.

The authority to undo

Rollback is the smallest gap here technically and the least settled in terms of authority. Once update exists, putting an agent back on its previous version is the same machinery pointed at an older digest.

The open question is who decides. A person, always, is safe and slow, and slow is a real cost when the thing being rolled back is producing bad work continuously. An agent deciding on its own needs a rule about what evidence is sufficient, and that rule does not exist.

What would have to exist: a statement of what evidence justifies a rollback, who may act on it, and how fast, and whether an agent may make that call about another agent.

What these have in common

Eight of the nine gaps are a missing agreement rather than a missing capability, and most of them come down to one agreement in different clothes: something written down before the work, by somebody other than the one doing it.

That is the contract before the code, the statement of what it may touch before the credentials are bound, the standard before the work, the address before the publication, and the evidence before the rollback. It is the oldest idea in engineering discipline and it turns out to be the thing that decides whether a stage can be handed to an agent.

Which is worth sitting with. The reason so much of a lifecycle cannot be handed over is not that the work is too hard for an agent. It is that the work was never specified well enough to hand to anyone.
