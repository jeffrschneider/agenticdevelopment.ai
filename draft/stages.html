The family of standards

Here are all twenty-four stages.

They run in seven phases, from deciding what's wanted to retiring the agent. Each one says what it's given, what it hands back, and how you can tell it's done. Take the ones you need, skip the ones you don't, and call the rest whatever you like.

  • Define
  • Make
  • Ship
  • Prove
  • Use
  • Run
  • Change
  • Beside the stages

Given and Hands back are the artifacts on either side of a stage. Done when is the check. Role is the job. Roles an agent can hold are published as contracts at agentroles.ai. Where a reference agent holds the role, its name sits beside the stage and links to its AgentDoc; where none does, the stage shows its role, ready for your agent or a person. Process names the phase of an example process at agentprocesses.ai that runs the stage.

Package and Release are the on-ramp. They're where your work becomes an Agent Package, which is the easy way onto the mesh. Deploy is marked goes live: it's where the agent starts, with its credentials and limits already in place.

Define

Requirements

01spec-writersolution-planner

Deciding what the thing is for. An agent built from this statement can't lean over and ask what you meant, so the statement has to say more than a human team would ever bother to write down. What the agent refuses matters as much as what it does.

Three things are never made up here, however tempting: the agent's name, its price, and its access to any system. Each one is a commitment somebody will be held to, so each one is asked for, and the statement goes out with the question open rather than quietly filled in.

Sometimes the ask is a solution: several agents doing one job together, each handing its result to the next. Then the solution planner holds this stage and Design. It writes down what the job is and what "done" means before anything runs, so the finished solution has something to be checked against. Any step no existing agent can do becomes a new agent, and the spec writer takes it from there.

Design

02spec-writersolution-planner

Turning the statement into a shape: the steps, in order, and for each one the decision that matters most in agentic work. Is this step a program, or a judgment? A written procedure handed to a model to improvise is the shape that fails, and it fails quietly, because nothing checks the procedure was followed.

The statement and the shape travel as one artifact, so the same agent holds both stages. Each step says when it's the right one, what a good result looks like, what happens when there's nothing to give, and how it's done.

Four questions get answered before any steps are drawn, because each changes what a later stage does, and all four are cheap to answer now and expensive to discover later. Which kind of agent is this, of the four an agent's inside can be? One agent or several? Work whose handoffs cross to another agent is a process, not one overloaded agent. Can the organization put a real product behind every system the design names? That's read against its own stack, so a design that can't be deployed is refused here rather than at the approval. And does the thing already exist, as an agent to contract, a repository to copy, or a released agent to change? The person picks from a short list each time, the simplest answer comes preselected, and the writer never answers on their behalf.

For a solution, the shape is the plan: the steps in order, what passes between them, and what each step needs. Which agent does each step gets decided at Source.

Impact

03role: impact analyst

Working out what a change touches before anything is built. The first time an agent is made, nothing depends on it and this stage has nothing to do. Every change after that lands on something people may already rely on: a solution that calls the agent, a schedule that runs it, a share somebody else is using. A change that's safe for its owner can break all of those.

So the change is compared with what runs now. For an agent, the old and new declarations are compared and every dependent is listed, both from what's declared, like the solutions that name it, and from what was seen, like who called it recently. Each dependent gets one of three answers. Compatible: the change adds something optional or widens a limit. Breaking: something the dependent sends or gets back changes. A change of behavior: the contract is the same, but the agent acts differently, which no declaration can show. For a solution, the old and new requirements and plans are compared step by step.

This half needs no model, because it's a comparison and a list. The other half, running the new version against what each dependent counts as done, needs the new version to exist, so it happens at Prove. Each breaking change and each change of behavior goes into the ledger as a debt owed to that dependent's owner.

Role
impact analyst
Given
the change, the version that runs now, and the list of what depends on it
Hands back
the impact report: each dependent and what the change does to it, what has to be redone, what can be reused, and what the change will cost
Done when
every dependent is named and given one of the three answers, and the person who asked approves or refuses the change from the report before anything is built

Source

04catalog

Finding what already exists before building anything new. Once the design has steps, each step needs something to do it, and the cheapest choice is something that already exists and has already been checked. The catalog is asked about each step, with what the step takes and hands back, and answers with an agent or solution that fits, or with "build one".

A catalog that always finds a match sends work to agents that don't fit. So the catalog has to be able to say "nothing fits", with the reason, and that answer is what sends a step off to be built.

Make

Test

05test-writer

Writing the tests before the program, from the statement of the job, by something that won't write the program. Running tests was solved decades ago. Writing them wasn't, and agentic work adds a twist: the same model often writes the program and the test, so a test can pass by agreeing with a mistake.

The fix is the order. The suite comes first, straight from the statement. It names the functions the program will have to export, so the program is written to meet an interface it didn't choose. That's why Test sits before Code here, whatever the older lifecycles got used to. (Yes, people argue about this. You can reorder yours.)

A suite written before any program exists gets checked in a particular way. It runs against an empty stand-in for every program the statement names, and it has to fail; a suite that passes with every function missing has asserted nothing. Once the programs exist, each is removed in turn and the suite has to notice. A program whose absence goes unnoticed is a program nothing tests, and it gets named.

Code

06code-writer

Writing the programs. Models write code well, and capability was never what held this stage back. What it needed was an input and a check: a statement of the job, and a suite written from it before any program exists. With those two in hand, writing code is a stage like any other, and the person who used to sit in the loop as the missing contract can step out of it.

This is the one stage where the model drives rather than being called at a named point. A coding harness works the way a coding session works: write, run the check, read the failures, fix, for as many rounds as it takes, up to a ceiling. What makes that safe is that none of the check belongs to the model. The suite passes. Each program, removed in turn, is noticed. The package maker takes what was written. And the tests and the statement are read-only to the writer, compared by digest every round, so whoever writes the program can't also change what checks it. A test that can't pass without breaking the statement is a conflict, reported for a person to settle.

Any code writer can hold this stage: the reference code-writer, your own agent, or a vendor's. Anything a person makes in their own tool, in any language, is a prototype, and making one is agentic prototyping. The Code stage reads the prototype as the most exact statement of what was wanted and writes the programs again against the suite, which is agentic transpiling. What leaves the stage is that rewrite, signed by the code writer that made it.

Review

07role: code critic

Reading the program and saying what's wrong with it. In every other setting a review is already an artifact: a list of findings, each naming a place and making a claim, each one answerable. For an agent to hold it, the findings need an agreed shape, and so does what it means to answer one.

The Code stage gives a reviewer plenty to read: what was written, and how many rounds it took. A reviewer should know when a program took twenty-eight rounds.

Role
code critic
Given
the programs and the tests, and the Code stage's account of what it did and how many rounds it took
Hands back
findings, each naming a place, a claim and a consequence
Done when
every finding is answered, by a change or by a stated reason

Security review

08role: security reviewer

Deciding what this thing may reach, what it may spend, and what it must never do, before it's given any of it. It's a stage of its own because the thing being built holds credentials and acts unattended, so the blast radius is set at design time and is very hard to shrink afterwards. A review after the credential is bound isn't a review. It's an audit.

Dependency and supply-chain vetting belongs here, not in Package. Packaging hashes what went into the package, which says what's in it, not whether it should be. The statement is checked against what the package says it will reach and the spending limit its owner set, before the agent is installed.

Role
security reviewer
Given
the shape, the programs, and everything the package will ask to be given
Hands back
a statement of what it may reach, what it may spend, what it must never do, and what was accepted knowingly
Done when
every access the package asks for has a reason recorded against it

Ship

Package

09the on-ramppackage-maker

Turning the shape and the programs into one thing that travels: an Agent Package. It holds the declaration, written as agent-descriptor.json and agent-runtime-requirements.json, plus the parts, a manifest and a digest. Building is a small part of this stage: it only generates the views and the digests. A package was an artifact long before agents showed up, which is why agents took to this stage so easily, and the declaration inside it is what the rest of the lifecycle reads.

The package maker turns your work into an Agent Package, and that's the easy way onto the mesh. Any tool can make the package, as long as the package is valid.

The package maker packs programs from any code writer. An organization that wants every program traced to one writer can switch that on: the reference package maker then packs only programs it can trace to a delivery that writer signed, and refuses the rest with a reply saying where to send them. The trace is the signed manifest every delivery already carries, so nothing new has to be signed.

Deploy

11goes livedeployment-manager

Installing the package, placing its credentials and setting its limits, all before it starts. The result has an identity of its own and an address other agents can find, so a deploy isn't finished when the files land. It's finished when somebody can write to it and get an answer.

There's never a moment when the agent is live without its credentials and its limits. They're in place before it starts.

Credentials are placed by name: a secret is named in the instruction and never carried in it, so the instruction can be read, logged and repeated without leaking anything. Every setting gets its value and everything that spends gets its ceiling. Then the agent starts, and the deployment manager signs a record of what it placed and checked.

Prove

Conformance

12conformance-checker

Checking that the running agent does what its package says. The cases come from the declaration rather than being written by hand, which makes the check cheap enough to run on every deploy. It answers one question, and answering it isn't the same as saying the work is good.

It's only half independent, and it's worth being plain about that. The declaration was written at Package, so a check derived from it catches an agent that doesn't do what it promised, and can't catch an agent that promised the wrong thing. The outside opinion is Eval, which is why the two are separate stages.

When the agent is a new version of one already running, this check runs on the new version while it's still a candidate, before it replaces the old one (see Update).

Acceptance

13a person

The person who asked decides the result is what they asked for. Conformance proves the agent does what its package says. This stage asks whether that's what was wanted, and only the person who asked can answer. It's a person's act on purpose.

The answer is checked against "done": what the result had to be, written down at Requirements before the work began. So the question isn't whether the person likes it, but whether it does what they said it must. The answer is recorded with who gave it and when, and it closes the request. For a solution, the result checked is its first run.

Role
the requester, the person who asked
Given
what was delivered, and "done" as it was written down at Requirements
Hands back
a recorded yes against "done", or a no that says what's missing
Done when
the answer is recorded with who gave it and when, and the request is closed

Docs

14documenterrole: doc writer

The document a stranger reads before deciding to use the thing. In agentic work it's assembled from what the agent already declares rather than written fresh. A document that's authored can say anything; a document that's gathered can only say what its sources say. Where a source is silent, the silence is printed.

Two roles share this stage. The documenter assembles and signs every AgentDoc, in the format defined at agentdoc.net. The doc writer writes the documentation people read to use or maintain the software, which has to be written rather than gathered, so it's a different job.

Publish

15publisher

Putting the output where the person who asked can open it. It looks like plumbing and isn't: publishing is the one stage that makes work visible outside the system, so it's the one stage where a mistake is public. It needs an authority to write to an address, a record of what was written, and a digest.

The agent holding it writes under the one folder it was bound to and nowhere else, reads every byte before it goes, refuses anything that looks like a credential whatever the file is called, and reads back what's served to check it matches what it was handed. Nothing counts as published until the address has been read.

Retract

16role: publisher

Taking down what was published, by the authority that put it there and no other. Publishing is the one stage whose output outlives the job that made it, so being able to publish without being able to undo it should be a deliberate choice. It's a stage of its own because the check is different: publishing is proven by reading the address and finding the bytes, retracting by reading it and finding nothing.

The record of every name the publisher placed is what tells it what it may take down. An address it never wrote isn't its to remove, however nicely it's asked.

Eval

18role: agent critic

Judging the work itself. Conformance says an agent answers; eval says whether the answer was any good. A mechanical check catches a fault but has no opinion about whether the work was worth doing. What eval needs is a written statement of what good looks like for this job, made before the work, by somebody other than the maker.

It sits in Run, not Prove, because it judges what an agent does on real jobs after it's installed, right beside the monitoring that says whether it answered at all.

Role
agent critic
Given
the work produced, and a standard set beforehand
Hands back
a graded judgment, with the reason on each grade
Done when
every piece is graded against the standard, and the standard predates the work

Incident response

19incident-recorder

What happens after monitoring finds something and before anybody decides to roll back. Detection isn't response, and the gap between them is where most of the damage gets done. A misbehaving agent keeps working: it has an inbox, it answers, and it turns out steadily worse output until somebody stops it. Stopping it is its own act, separate from fixing it.

The agent holding this stage records the incident and does nothing else. It reads the platform's records and the failed agent's own records around the failure, and writes a timeline of what's known, with each gap marked as a gap. It never restarts anything, never guesses a cause, and never proposes a fix. Deciding what to do stays a person's call.

Change

Update

21deployment-manager

Moving a running agent to a new version of its package while it keeps its identity, its address and its history. The version changes; the subject doesn't. What changed has to be written down, because the agent's own document will be read by people who weren't there for the change.

The stage asks for three recorded steps. Candidate: the new version is installed beside the old one, under the same identity, and answers only the messages sent to test it. Prove: the conformance check runs against it, and so does what each dependent counts as done. Promote: the identity moves to the new version, and the old one stays installed but stopped for a while, in case it's needed back. Before the promote, the ledger is read, and an open debt that blocks the promote stops it.

The code is rarely the risky part. The records an agent already wrote are in the old version's shapes, and the new version has to keep reading them: the ordinary migration problem, turning up somewhere unfamiliar.

Reconfigure

22deployment-manager

Changing a running agent's settings, credentials or ceilings without a new version. A rotated key, a lowered spending limit, a new value for a setting: the agent keeps its version, its identity and its address, and nothing about the package changes.

The same rule as Deploy holds: a secret is named, never carried. And every change leaves a signed record of what changed, who asked, and when, so there is no guessing later why the agent started behaving differently on a Tuesday.

Rollback

23role: deployment manager

Putting a running agent back on the version before, quickly, when the new one is wrong. Moving the code back is the easy part. The real question is authority: who may decide, on what evidence, how fast, and whether one agent may make that call about another without waiting for a person.

Moving the code back also isn't undoing the work. A version that ran for an hour has sent messages, spent money, written files and changed data somebody else now depends on, and none of that reverses when the version does. A rollback that only names the version leaves the mess behind.

When an update keeps the old version installed for a while after the promote, rolling back is the promote run in reverse, with the reason recorded.

Role
deployment manager
Given
the agent, and the version it was on before
Hands back
the same agent back on that version, the reason it was moved, and what the bad version did that can't be undone
Done when
the agent is answering on the old version, the reason is recorded, and the side effects are named

Beside the stages

The ledger

kept by the platform

Some of what a stage finds is owed rather than handed on. A change that breaks something another person's work depends on leaves a debt to that person. A problem found and left unfixed is a debt too, owed to ourselves. Neither belongs to one stage, and both get lost when they live in somebody's notes.

The ledger holds both, under one numbering. Change dependency debt is owed to the owner of something that depends on what changed. It has a deadline, and until it's settled the changed agent can't be promoted, rolled back or retired. Technical debt is owed to ourselves, and it blocks nothing. Any stage can add an item, and the deployment manager reads the ledger before it acts. The ledger calls no model and judges nothing. It numbers what it's told, records who changed each item, when and why, and never deletes one.

Kind
a record the platform keeps, not an agent: agents and people call it, and it holds no role
Given
an item: what it's against, who it's owed to, what caused it, and what it blocks
Hands back
the item with its number and, when asked, the open items that block an act
Checked by
no number is ever given twice, every change to an item says who made it and when, and no item is ever deleted
